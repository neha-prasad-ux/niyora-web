Most people have heard of PMS. Far fewer have heard of PMDD, and that gap leaves a lot of women wondering if what they feel before their period is normal, or something more. Here is the plain-language difference, and the honest guide to when it is worth talking to a doctor.

This is educational, not a diagnosis. Only a clinician can tell you what you have. What this can do is help you know what you are looking at, and when to ask for help.

## What PMS is

PMS, premenstrual syndrome, is the set of physical and emotional changes many women feel in the days before their period. Around 80 percent of women feel some premenstrual symptoms. It can look like irritability, feeling low or tearful, anxiety, tender breasts, bloating, or trouble sleeping.

The key thing about ordinary PMS: it is real, and it is manageable. It shows up with the hormonal shift and eases with the next one. It can be uncomfortable, but most weeks it does not stop you from living your life, holding your relationships, or doing your work.

## What PMDD is

PMDD, premenstrual dysphoric disorder, is a severe, diagnosable condition. It affects roughly 5 to 8 percent of women. It is not just stronger PMS in feeling, it is different in how much it takes over.

The difference is severity and disruption. With PMDD, the emotional symptoms are intense enough to seriously disrupt life. Think relationships strained to breaking, work or study that becomes very hard to manage, and a depth of low mood, anxiety, or anger that feels overwhelming. For some, it comes with hopelessness or thoughts of self-harm. The symptoms follow the cycle tightly: they arrive in the days before the period and lift within a few days of it starting, then the person feels like themselves again.

PMDD is not weakness or a character flaw. It was only recognized as a full medical diagnosis in 2013. Before that, many women waited years and saw several doctors before anyone named it. If no one has explained this to you, that is medicine catching up slowly, not you overreacting.

## How to tell them apart

There is no home test, and this is not a checklist to diagnose yourself with. But in plain terms, the questions a doctor is weighing are:

- **How severe is it?** PMS is uncomfortable. PMDD is disabling. If the emotional symptoms regularly overwhelm you, that is the line worth paying attention to.
- **How much does it disrupt your life?** If relationships, work, or your sense of yourself take a serious hit every cycle, that matters.
- **Does it track your cycle tightly?** PMDD symptoms cluster in the days before your period and lift soon after it starts. Tracking how you feel across a couple of cycles is the single most useful thing you can bring to a doctor.

## When to see a doctor

If what you feel before your period is severe, if it seriously disrupts your life, your relationships, or your work, or if the low moods run deep, it is worth talking to a doctor. This is the honest version: if this sounds like you, please talk to a clinician. PMDD is real, and it is treatable. There are options that help, and you do not have to white-knuckle through it every month.

Tracking your symptoms across two full cycles, noting what you feel and where it falls relative to your period, gives a doctor the clearest picture and often shortens the path to help.

If you ever feel hopeless, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out now. In the US, you can call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time. If you are somewhere else, your local emergency number or a crisis line can help. You deserve support, and it is there.
